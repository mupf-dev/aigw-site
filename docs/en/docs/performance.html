Performance and load comparison

Test setup and results of the latency and load tests of ai-gw, plus a comparison with the LiteLLM proxy under identical conditions – with method, raw values and the limits of what the results show.

Adapted from: Operations manual “Performance” and measurement logs (November 2026)

  3. Load tests

A gateway sits in front of every model request. What it costs – latency, CPU, memory – is paid by every application. ai-gw therefore measures this regularly with its own scripts, included in the repository. The figures on this page come from runs in November 2026.

Context. Measurements were taken against a test upstream that responds immediately. With real model providers, with response times from hundreds of milliseconds to seconds, the overhead of any gateway is small; what matters then is mainly CPU per request, memory, throughput per core and behavior under many concurrent streams.

Test setup#

  • Machine: 8 vCPU (AMD EPYC, virtualized), 16 GB RAM, Linux. The machine was not exclusive during the measurements; all measurement processes therefore ran at real-time priority on fixed cores. Absolute values vary between runs by 20 to 30 percent; ratios within a run are reliable.
  • Separate cores: test upstream (OpenAI-compatible, JSON and SSE) on two cores, the gateway on two cores, the load generator (keep-alive) on the rest.
  • Realistic chain without plugins: API key check, organization, team and key with limits and budgets, cost calculation via a price, request log enabled, routing with active retries and circuit breakers.
  • Overhead = gateway minus a direct call to the upstream in the same scenario (percentiles subtracted from one another).

Gateway latency#

With the native binary, the overhead on a single connection was 80 to 92 µs (p50) and under 200 µs (p99), for JSON as well as for time-to-first-token on streams. The target “p99 under 1 ms” is met up to 2,000 requests per second over 200 connections (overhead p99 140 to 380 µs). At 10,000 requests per second on only two cores, the overhead rises to 1.3 to 1.7 ms (p99); according to the measurements, this rate needs around four cores for p99 under 1 ms.

Load tests#

Long streams (200 chunks at 5 ms intervals) over 1,000 and 4,000 concurrent connections, gateway still on two cores:

  • No errors and no aborted streams, even with 4,000 concurrent streams. Under overload, backpressure shows up as longer stream duration, not as memory growth.
  • Memory stable: 178 to 187 MiB over three identical rounds; around 85 KiB per open stream (client and upstream connection including buffers).
  • CPU per chunk around 19 µs, mostly system calls; JSON saturation with 1,000 connections around 22,500 requests per second.
  • For sustained load, Postgres is recommended over SQLite, because the request log and budget reconciliation share a write lock on SQLite.

Comparison with LiteLLM#

LiteLLM is one of the models for ai-gw and a widely used, feature-rich open-source gateway. The comparison is meant to show how the different technical approaches (Rust with in-memory configuration versus Python) play out under identical conditions. It is not an overall assessment: feature scope, ecosystem and operational experience are not reflected in the figures.

Interpretation#

  • On the same two cores, the overhead of ai-gw on a single connection was lower by a factor of 25 to 35, the throughput at saturation higher by a factor of 60 to 230 and the CPU per request lower by a factor of 100 to 150. The differences are considerably larger than the fluctuations of the measurement machine.
  • From 100 connections, LiteLLM did not complete all requests (connection drops, with Postgres at 500 connections additionally 503); ai-gw remained error-free in all runs.
  • The overhead of ai-gw in the container (around 0.3 ms) is above that of the native binary (around 0.09 ms) – causes are the container cgroup, the shared machine and wake-up costs at low rates.

Limits of the results#

  • Test upstream without real model time; OpenAI entry point only, no plugins, guardrails or cache.
  • Only two cores and one instance per gateway. LiteLLM scales horizontally via further workers and instances (according to its documentation one vCPU and at least 4 GiB per worker); more workers, Redis and other ASGI servers were not measured.
  • LiteLLM without a database checks only the master key and therefore has less to do than the ai-gw chain; the functionally comparable variant is LiteLLM with Postgres.
  • The machine was not exclusive; absolute figures vary, the ratios are reliable.

Measure it yourself#

The scripts are in the repository under scripts/ (bench-e2e.sh, bench-latency.sh, bench-coldstart.sh, bench-litellm.sh) and write their results to target/bench/. The raw logs of the runs shown here are under docs/betrieb/messwerte/. For reliable absolute figures, an exclusive machine is recommended.
